Book miniatures: Wallace, Consider the Lobster

Of course I didn’t stop reading. I just went back to reading one of my favorite authors, which helped a bit.

I will focus on one essay in this collection, ‘Authority and American Usage.’ It is a review of Bryan A. Garner’s A Dictionary of Modern American Usage, and more broadly a discussion of linguistic prescriptivism – i.e., people telling you how to use words and how not to use words. As Wallace points out, in linguistics as an academic field, prescriptivism is largely regarded with disdain, if not scorn. Most linguists, including myself, identify as descriptivists: we are interested in how people actually use language and in the implicit knowledge that gives rise to this usage, not in some artificial system of arbitrary rules such as ‘never split infinitives’ that society has invented just to make life difficult, or at least so it seems. Wallace argues against this anti-prescriptivist attitude in a way that I found largely convincing. While conceding that some stylistic dogmas are ill-founded and can be safely ignored – including the one on split infinitives –, he makes the case that prescriptivism has its place in society, for three reasons.

1. Prescriptivism is about clarity

This is the most obvious argument, and even the most hardcore descriptivists would concede this point. Some prescriptive rules simply have the goal of making prose easier to parse for the reader. Avoiding adjunct attachment ambiguities and center embeddings isn’t a matter of stylistic niceties, but of clearly communicating the idea you want to express.

2. Prescriptivism is about respect

This is a more interesting point. Wallace argues that the principles of good writing – including, on the most basic level, the rules of punctuation and orthography – are indeed arbitrary societal conventions, but so is, say, shaking hands when meeting somebody. It is a practice that is perfectly contingent, it is limited to certain societies, and there is no deeper reason why it should exist; nonetheless, ignoring it is plainly rude. A couple of years ago, I regularly texted with someone I worked with in a climate activism group, who refused to use any capitalization (in German, where every noun should be capitalized) and whose messages were generally riddled with typos and errors, making it clear that he did not spend a single second proofreading them before hitting send. His style of writing communicated that his time was far too valuable to bother about such trivial matters as orthography (he was one of the leading figures in the group) and that he could not care less about the impression his writing made on me. I found this absolutely infuriating. Rejecting societal norms for the sole reason that they are arbitrary is not a bold revolutionary stance; it is childish. The reason to follow societal conventions is not that they are well-motivated, grounded in some biological necessity, logical reasoning, deeper ethical obligations, or anything of that kind, which they are obviously not, but that this is the only way for us to not drive each other crazy all the time.

3. Prescriptivism is about getting what you want

This last argument I found the weakest, and it is, in a sense, cynical. Wallace is perfectly clear about the fact that the language prescribed by people like Garner is not some kind of meta-societal Esperanto, but the language of a very particular social group, viz., affluent white people. The goal of teaching prescriptivist grammar to people outside of this group – as Wallace did as a college professor – is thus to help them advance in society. I see his point, and I do not strictly morally object to it, but it is such an obviously unsatisfying solution to a genuine problem.

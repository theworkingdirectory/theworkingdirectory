Book miniatures: Beard, Confronting the Classics

Confronting the Classics by Mary Beard, a Cambridge professor and one of the biggest names in Classics these days, is a collection of short, previously published book reviews. In other words, it is the kind of book you can only get published if you are such a bona fide academic superstar as Beard is, who is brilliant and entertaining as hell and, for all I can tell, just a very cool person. She also has perfected the art of the book review: critical but fair, detailed but not digressive, funny but not mean. In the preface, she states her one rule for book reviews: never write something you wouldn’t say to a person’s face. As someone who has recently started to sometimes meet researchers in real life whose work I have criticized in writing, I found this healthy advice.

But what is much more difficult is to be at the receiving end of reviews, and if there is one aspect of being in academia that I loathe, this is it. Reviews can be gut-wrenchingly painful. There is no way to disentangle the critique of the research from the critique of the researcher; even if it is not ad hominem, it is always personal as long as you care about your research, which I do. It is of course true that constructive feedback – which not all feedback is, but let’s leave that aside – genuinely improves and advances your research, but I don’t want to improve; I want to already be great. And so what stood out to me in Confronting the Classics is that with the exception of one raving review of a massive monograph on bilingualism in the late Roman Empire, Beard finds flaws and shortcomings with every single book she writes about. I do not take this to mean that she is petty or fussy or overly critical, but as a reminder that this is what research is like: always flawed, always falling short in one way or another.

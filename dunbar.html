Book miniatures: Dunbar, Friends

There is a growing field of research showing that an active social life is at least as important for our health and well-being as sleep, exercise, a healthy diet and the other usual suspects. Dunbar’s book assembles a wealth of evidence for this claim, as well as on the nature of human (and occasionally non-human) social relationships more in general. I had some trepidations about some of the studies cited, but Dunbar is clearly an expert in his field and the data are solid overall.

One of the questions that Dunbar is particularly interested in is how many friends people tend to have and how close they are to them. Apparently, people tend to have one or two extremely close friends (typically a romantic partner and/or a BFF), three to five best friends, eight to ten additional close friends, etc. I was genuinely surprised by how high these numbers are. It turns out that statistically speaking, the number of friends I have is exceedingly low, which I was always vaguely aware of but not in that much numerical detail. I can’t name more than eight people who I choose to spend time with one-on-one on an even remotely regular basis, whether in person, on the phone or on zoom (excluding most family members – they are not a matter of choice), and I will almost certainly lose some of them now that I have moved to another country. I have thirty people in my phone’s contacts list. My arguably best friend is someone who I haven’t seen in person for years and who I talk to about every two or three months, which according to Dunbar’s system would place him in the third layer of friends at best (close friends but not best friends), leaving the two innermost layers completely empty for me. In other words, I should be miserable.

But that just doesn’t seem right. I never feel lonely (unless I’m around other people, interestingly). I have no particular desire to talk to my best friend more than a couple of times a year. I’m quite picky in the kind of people I tolerate around me, and I can’t think of anyone who I know and who I would like to be friends with but currently am not. I enjoy being alone, and I don’t think that recruiting more friends would increase my life expectancy, as Dunbar repeatedly claims. It just feels like an unnecessary effort.

So I’m obviously slightly offended, and I don’t think that it is Dunbar’s job to have opinions about the number of friends I have. But more in general, it is noticeable that none of the studies Dunbar cites seem to be at all concerned with inter-subject variation, and the book eventually only offers blanket prescriptions (five best friends each, to be taken on a weekly basis, ask your doctor about any risks and side effects), which seems suspect. Here’s a parallel. It is well-established that a healthy diet is an integral part of a healthy lifestyle, and yet there is fairly little nutritional guidance that would apply to (almost) everyone – eat mostly plants, drink water when you’re thirsty, etc. Anything more specific than that – aim for 2000 calories a day, eat more protein, drink celery juice… – just is not solid advice on a population level, for the simple reason that people have different bodies and do different things. By the same token, it is not clear to me that all of the research in this book supports any population-level advice other than: spend time with other people sometimes. Maybe talk to them. When you feel lonely, try to do something about it. That’s all.
